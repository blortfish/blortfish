Critique 4

By Daniel Flint

Breaks in Attention

In class we have discussed divided attention. How in some cases you may not do as well as you could have unless you were focused on that one task. This is true for many times in a college student's day. Texting during class, daydreaming, perhaps both. One of the biggest problems I have as a college student is staying focused on one task at a time. Not to say that it is aimed in only one direction or another, but I have two computer monitors and that leaves plenty of room for Microsoft Word and a browser window or two to be up at the same time. It is excellent when working on programming or web development, but it is just as distracting because of Facebook, texting, YouTube, and other time wasting websites that are out there. It could be prevented by closing everything other than exactly what you need. Also, music is a must for me while doing work. Switching song to song on YouTube can be time consuming and distracting. If I were to use an iTunes playlist in the background it may be less distracting.

Another time that I find I can become easily distracted is while driving. Night time driving is sometimes difficult for me. It can be hard to see and may pull me in one direction or another when I look at lights. Oncoming traffic is very distracting to me focusing on where I am supposed to be on the road. Also sometimes there are street lights or house lights that offer other distractions. This is a type of perceptual conspicuity. The other lights are distracting because of their size or color as compared to other objects which are all black at night. Something that could be done to prevent this is drive in a more well lit environment such as a city, or simply try not to drive as much at night because it is possibly dangerous.

Memory failure

I will admit that a large amount of the time I struggle with short term memory. Phone numbers, names, any small piece of data that should seem easy to remember. It seems that I fall victim to the serial position effect, the primacy, and the recency effect. I always seem to be able to remember the first few digits and the last few. This is most likely because the beginning is the easiest to rehearse. Also, being that your auditory memory lasts about five seconds, I can recall that pretty easily. However, unless I write it down or put the number into a contact list, its seems I will never overcome this problem. The only ways to fix this problem would be perhaps try to chunk data more effectively or just write it down.

We have discussed visibility in class as well. In memory, you can only remember what you see for about a second. That is, in the short term. Looking at a computer, all of the GUI programs and pictures, it seems that I have developed a general model of what buttons "should" look like. It has also become alot easier to navigate a GUI based directory system. Visibility illustrates that fact. If you were to ask me where a file was located on my computer right now, I would essentially flip through which folders I know the file is in and what is underneath that. If you look at an older DOS type file system, or in my case an ftp or ssh into a file server, it is much harder for me to recall which directory it would be located in. It has to do with the visibility and what you see within a directory. A file server would make you type to see what is within the directory and gives you much more information than necessary in general.
